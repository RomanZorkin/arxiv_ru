Abstract

This paper develops a deep learning-based framework for pricing convertible bonds with path-dependent contractual features, namely downward conversion price reset and issuer call clauses under rolling-window trigger rules, which are widespread in the convertible bond market. We formulate the valuation problem as a path-dependent partial differential equation (PPDE), which explicitly captures the dependence of the convertible bond value on the historical path of the underlying asset and the dynamic evolution of the conversion price. We derive consistent PPDE formulations for three canonical underlying dynamics: geometric Brownian motion (GBM), constant elasticity of variance (CEV) and Heston stochastic volatility. We then construct a discrete-time dynamic programming scheme in which conditional expectations are approximated by neural networks, which remains tractable in such high-dimensional path-dependent setting. Empirical tests on China CITIC Bank Convertible Bond show that our framework produces stable and accurate prices and sensitivity patterns across all model specifications. Three key economic insights emerge: 1. Contractual features dominate underlying dynamics in determining convertible bond values. 2. The call provision decreases convertible bonds prices by truncating upside gains. 3. Counterintuitively, despite improving conversion terms, the downward reset provision further decreases the price of convertible bonds by lowering the effective call threshold and making early redemption more likely. The proposed PPDE-deep learning approach provides an efficient, flexible tool for pricing convertible bonds with complex path-dependent structures.

Keywords: convertible bonds, path-dependent provisions, path-dependent partial differential equation, artificial neural networks, least squares Monte Carlo.

1 Introduction

Convertible bonds (CBs) are hybrid financial instruments that give bondholders the right, but not the obligation, to convert the bond into a fixed number of the issuer’s common shares at a predetermined conversion price under specific terms, either prior to maturity or forced redemption by the issuer. Until conversion or maturity, holders retain the cash flow rights of a conventional bond, including periodic coupon payments and principal repayment at maturity. As a typical financial instrument, CBs possess both the fixed-income attribute of conventional bonds for downside protection and the equity upside attribute of issuer’s stocks, and offer an asymmetric payoff structure: when the stock price performs weakly, their bond characteristics provide a meaningful downside protection for investors; while when the underlying stock price rises, investors can participate in the gains from the growth through voluntary conversion. From a corporate financing perspective, CBs reduce initial equity dilution relative to direct equity issuance and lower coupon costs compared to ordinary bonds, making them a widely used financing tool globally.

Most real-world CBs embed two critical path-dependent provisions: an issuer call provision, which allows the issuer to redeem the bonds early at a contractually specified call price once a rolling-window price trigger is met; and a downward conversion price reset, in which the conversion price is reduced if the stock underperforms over a rolling window, partially protecting investors on the downside. These clauses introduce strong nonlinearity and path-dependence. The reset feature is particularly prevalent in Chinese CBs, yet poses severe challenges to traditional pricing methods.

The valuation of CBs has been an important topic in financial engineering due to this inherent complexity. Existing methods fall into three broad categories: closed-form solutions, partial differential equation (PDE)-based numerical methods, and simulation-based approaches. An early closed-form approach is Ingersoll’s contingent-claim pricing model (Ingersoll, 1977), in which the author extended the Black-Scholes option pricing framework to CBs pricing with dilution effects. (Ingersoll, 1977) provides a structural approach for convertible bond pricing with embedded options, which can be viewed as an option determined by the value of the firm. Subsequent studies (Lewis, 1991; Nyborg, 1996; Zhu, 2006) developed closed-form solutions under optimal conversion strategies only at maturity (Nyborg, 1996) and any time before maturity (Zhu, 2006). These closed-form expressions cannot accommodate realistic path-dependent features.

Second, PDE-based methods formulate CB pricing as a system of variational inequalities, solved numerically via lattice-based methods or finite difference methods (Brennan and Schwartz, 1977, 1980; Tsiveriotis and Fernandes, 1998; Ayache et al., 2003). Brennan and Schwartz (1977) proposed PDEs governing the value of the CB with boundary conditions and incorporated stochastic interest rate (Brennan and Schwartz, 1980). Tsiveriotis and Fernandes (1998) developed a coupled PDE system for the equity component under the standard Black-Scholes model and the debt component accounting for credit risk. Ayache et al. (2003) unified the aforementioned coupled model into a single PDE framework. Lau and Kwok (2004) considered soft call and conversion condition with delayed calls. Lin and Zhu (2020) used an ADI-based method to solve the PDEs with stochastic volatility and interest rates. Lim (2026) proposed a structured framework for resettable convertible bonds.

Following the foundational binomial tree approach in option pricing (Cox et al., 1979) and the bivariate tree method for two-factor models (Hull and White, 1994), lattice extensions such as binomial trees (Ammann et al., 2003) and trinomial trees (Xu et al., 2009) have also been used for CB pricing. Ma and Xu (2020) proposed a hybrid willow tree method to consider soft call and put provisions. Recently, Costabile and Viviano (2026) combined lattice with regression techniques for path-dependent soft call and put provisions. Other PDE approaches, such as the finite element method, have also been used for CB pricing (Barone-Adesi et al., 2003). However, these methods are not well suited to jointly handle early exercise and reset features, stochastic volatility, and the rolling‑window path dependence that characterize Chinese CBs due to high dimensionality.

Third, simulation-based methods, particularly the least‑squares Monte Carlo (LSMC) method (Carriere, 1996; Longstaff and Schwartz, 2001), are more flexible for realistic dynamics of state variables and CB’s path dependence features under the dynamic programming framework (Ammann et al., 2008; Kimura and Shinohara, 2006; Yang et al., 2010). Li et al. (2025a) considered call/put and reset clauses, approximated the conditional expectation approximation using quadratic basis functions under the LSMC framework while using constant volatility and Markovian trigger conditions.

Recently, a few articles applied machine learning methods to convertible bonds pricing. Zhu et al. (2024) developed a learning based Monte Carlo simulation method to replace ordinary least squares regression without considering contractual provisions. Neural-network-based data-driven models such as financial time-series synthetic data generation have also been used to simulate the returns of underlying stocks. Tan et al. (2022) simulated stock returns using time-series generative adversarial networks (GANs) to capture their fat-tail properties for valuation. Li et al. (2025a) combined GANs with transformers to generate stock returns. So far, the existing learning-based approaches do not focus on optimal decision policies estimation, ignore joint provisions, and remain low-dimensional.

Despite extensive development of theoretical CB pricing models, two critical gaps still remain. Empirical evidence consistently documents significant pricing discrepancies in real markets (Ammann et al., 2003; Zabolotnyuk et al., 2010). Using samples of pure convertible bonds and real transaction data, recent studies show that convertible bonds are systematically underpriced, with mispricing driven by factors such as stochastic volatility, liquidity, and market conditions (Ammann et al., 2003). In particular, pricing errors are more pronounced during market stress periods and for bonds with higher credit risk and lower liquidity (Batten et al., 2018; Li et al., 2025b).

Incorporating embedded features like call and reset clauses is also difficult, especially when these features are path-dependent. Earlier work, such as Nyborg (1996), only considered non-callable bond for simplicity. The call provision is a typical feature granting the issuer the right of redemption prior to maturity under certain conditions, while the investor has the right to convert the CB into equities upon and before calling.

Hard call provisions, representing a fixed non-callable period during which the issuer cannot redeem the bond, have been considered (Tsiveriotis and Fernandes, 1998). Soft call provisions further require the stock price to meet certain triggering conditions: having the stock price to exceed a predefined trigger price (Brennan and Schwartz, 1977; Ayache et al., 2003), or to remain above a threshold for a specific period of time, e.g., 15 days, or for a fraction of a window, e.g., 15 days out of 30 days, also called mm-out-of-nn soft call provision under a moving window setting, also known as Parisian feature.

Early classical frameworks, including Brennan and Schwartz (1977); Ayache et al. (2003); Lau and Kwok (2004), focus only on path-independent soft call/put provisions. While common in CB markets, path-dependent mm-out-of-nn Parisian-style soft-call provisions are difficult to handle with traditional numerical methods. For instance, Lau and Kwok (2004) noted that the computational cost of their finite-difference method approach grows exponentially with the length of the moving window. Liu and Guo (2020) used a Monte Carlo method to approximate the call activation probability, triggering the provision once the probability exceeds 50%. Both methods have to resort to crude approximations which yield biased bond prices. In Ma and Xu (2020), the soft call activation probability was estimated using Brownian bridge simulations. The recent article Costabile and Viviano (2026) improved the probability estimation of call activation at each time step, using a proxy function constructed by the Brownian bridge estimation under the classical LSMC framework with polynomial basis functions (Longstaff and Schwartz, 2001) of the stock price and interest rate regressors.

Another stream of literature concentrates exclusively on conversion price reset clauses. For example, Kimura and Shinohara (2006) priced non-callable convertible bonds with reset downwards clauses by estimating the conversion probability. Zhu et al. (2018) used an integral equation approach for resettable convertible bonds. Cen et al. (2024) used decomposition and PDE methods for American-style resettable CBs. Costabile and Viviano (2026) considered the mm-out-of-nn soft call and put provisions. Few models jointly incorporate both call and put clauses and downward resets at the same time. Feng et al. (2016) proposed a decomposition-based model, which requires explicit classification of price paths into discrete regimes. Li et al. (2025a) considered call/put and reset clauses and estimated the solution under the classical LSMC framework.

These limitations are especially acute for Chinese CBs, which universally use rolling-window triggers for both resets and calls. Compared to conventional CBs in Europe and the United States (Costabile and Viviano, 2026), Chinese CBs exhibit distinctive path-dependent call and reset clauses (Li et al., 2025a), making bond values highly sensitive to the historical trajectory of the stock price and triggering events. In particular, market expectations regarding potential conversion price revisions and issuer-forced redemptions can materially affect valuation. Consequently, the strong path-dependent feature make them less amenable to conventional low-dimensional pricing methods. Moreover, the sensitivity of model outputs to inputs, such as volatility dynamics and credit spreads, further contributes to the discrepancies between model-implied values and market prices. Therefore, advanced valuation models must carefully incorporate both path dependence and the dynamics of state variables.

Motivated by these challenges, in this paper, we develop a deep learning‑based numerical framework for pricing CBs with both path‑dependent downward reset and call provisions. We formulate the valuation problem as a path‑dependent partial differential equation (PPDE), which explicitly captures dependence on the historical stock price path and the dynamic evolution of the conversion price. Within this unified framework, we derive consistent PPDE formulations under three canonical dynamics: geometric Brownian motion (GBM), constant elasticity of variance (CEV), and Heston stochastic volatility. We then construct a backward discrete‑time dynamic programming scheme and use DNNs to approximate the conditional expectations that would otherwise remain intractable in such a high‑dimensional path‑dependent setting. We also perform a convergence analysis of this numerical scheme. Empirically, we use the CITIC Bank Convertible Bond, a representative large Chinese CB with typical reset and call triggers, and obtain stable, accurate prices across all dynamic specifications.

Beyond the methodological contribution, our analysis delivers several consistent economic insights. The value of the convertible bond is driven primarily by the underlying stock price, exhibiting a pronounced nonlinear and convex pattern characteristic of embedded option features. Volatility has a positive but moderate effect, constrained by the bond floor on the downside, and by the call provision on the upside. In fact, the contractual design matters more than the choice of underlying dynamics: the call provision reduces value by truncating upside gains, while the downward reset provision, despite improving conversion terms, further lowers value by reducing the effective call threshold and increasing early redemption likelihood. These patterns hold uniformly across GBM, CEV, and Heston models.

In summary, the three key contributions of our paper are the following:

  1. 1.

    Theoretically, we are the first to formulate CB pricing with joint downward resets and path‑dependent calls as a PPDE, capturing historical price dependence and conversion price dynamics in a unified framework.

  2. 2.

    Numerically, we propose a backward deep learning algorithm that efficiently solves high‑dimensional path‑dependent CB valuation via neural network approximation of conditional expectations, with rigorous convergence guarantees. Its main novelty lies in the design of the one-step operator and state representation, which allow us to better and more accurately incorporate rolling-window path-dependent contractual features such as reset and call provisions within a PPDE-consistent framework.

  3. 3.

    In the empirical analysis, using China CITIC Bank convertible bond as an example, we show that contractual features play a more important role in valuation than the specification of the underlying dynamics. The call provision reduces the bond value by truncating the upside potential, which is consistent with intuition. By contrast, the downward reset provision, despite improving the conversion terms, lowers the effective call threshold and increases the likelihood of early redemption, and thus further reduces the bond value. This is a key counterintuitive finding.

The structure of the paper is as follows: we introduce the path-dependent pricing framework in Section 2. The deep learning numerical scheme and its convergence properties are established in Section 3. Detailed proofs of all theoretical results are collected in Appendices A and B. The empirical performance of the method is examined in Section 4. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper.

2 A path-dependent pricing framework

This section formulates the pricing of convertible bonds with downward reset and call provisions as a path-dependent problem, leading to a path-dependent partial differential equation (PPDE) representation, and derives the corresponding equations under GBM, CEV, and Heston dynamics.

2.1 PPDE representation

A convertible bond can be viewed as a hybrid security that combines a straight bond component with embedded equity conversion option. At any time tt, the payoff of the contract depends on the current stock price StS_{t} and the prevailing conversion price HtH_{t}. If held to maturity TT, the bondholder receives the maximum between the face value of bond BB and the conversion value (B/HT)​ST(B/H_{T})S_{T}, resulting in the terminal payoff

max⁡(B,BHT​ST).\max\!\left(B,\frac{B}{H_{T}}S_{T}\right).

In practice, most convertible bonds incorporate two critical path-dependent provisions: issuer call and downward reset clauses. The call provision grants the issuer the right to redeem the bond once certain path-dependent trigger conditions are met, which effectively caps the upside potential for the bondholders. The redemption call price KtK_{t} is normally the par value BB plus accrued interest. On the other hand, the downward reset provision allows a reduction in the conversion price HtH_{t} following a prolonged poor stock price performance, which improves the conversion value and offer investors partial downside protection.

A distinctive feature of these contractual provisions is that the triggering conditions depend on how the underlying stock price evolves over time, rather than only its current level. In practice, the triggers are typically determined by rolling-windows rules: for instance, whether the stock price has stayed above or below a specific proportion of the conversion price for a consecutive number of trading days. As a result, the value of the contract is determined not only by the current state variables (St,Ht)(S_{t},H_{t}), but also by the historical path of the stock price. As a representative example, the China CITIC Bank convertible bond (code: 113021) is one of the largest bank-issued convertible bonds in the Chinese market, with an issuance size of approximately RMB 40 billion. It is a standard large-sized convertible security with a fixed conversion price and six-year maturity (from 4 March 2019 to 3 March 2025), which allows bondholders to convert the bond into a predetermined number of shares during the conversion period. In addition to this standard conversion feature, the contract incorporates typical path-dependent adjustment and redemption mechanisms that are widely adopted in the Chinese convertible bond market (McGuinness and Keasey, 2010; Zhang and Zhu, 2023).

In particular, for this CITIC Bank CB, the conversion reset is activated when the stock price stays below 80% of the current conversion price for at least 15 trading days within any consecutive 30 trading days. On the other side, when the stock price exceeds 130% of the conversion price for at least 15 trading days within a 30-day rolling window, the issuer has the right to call the bond before maturity. These path-dependent provisions are designed to balance the interests of investors and issuers: the reset clause provides downside protection by adjusting the conversion price downward, while the call provision enables the issuer to force conversion when the equity option becomes sufficiently deep in the money. To simplify the analysis and focus on the joint path-dependent effects of call and reset provisions, we adopt the following assumptions.

Assumption 1.

For the pricing framework developed in this paper, we impose the following assumptions:

  1. 1.

    Under the anti-dilution provisions for the conversion price, the underlying stock pays no dividends and no rights issues or share placements occur over the life of the convertible bond.

  2. 2.

    Prior to the activation of the call provision, early conversion is not optimal. Once the call condition is satisfied, the issuer has the right, but not the obligation, to redeem the bond, and the holder has the right, but not the obligation, to convert the bond to stocks.

  3. 3.

    A downward reset is triggered when the stock price declines to the predefined reset threshold. For analytical tractability, the revised conversion price is set equal to the stock price at the threshold.

  4. 4.

    Credit risk is omitted in the baseline model to isolate and emphasize the impact of contractual path dependence. Also as the vast majority of Chinese CBs come with guarantees provided by high-credit-rated banks, credit risks associated with China’s CBs can be reasonably excluded.

  5. 5.

    Under the risk-neutral measure, the underlying stock price follows an arbitrage-free stochastic process. We consider three specifications: GBM, CEV, and the Heston models.

Under these assumptions, the contractual provisions explicitly depend on the historical evolution of the underlying stock price, rendering the valuation problem inherently path-dependent. Remark 1.

The above formulation captures the essential contractual features of Chinese convertible bonds. The call provision introduces an early termination condition, while the downward reset clause introduces a regime-switching structure that endogenously updates the conversion price upon trigger activation. Since both trigger events depend on the historical stock price path, the valuation problem naturally leads to a path-dependent partial differential equation (PPDE) framework.

Remark 2.

Regarding the continuation value situation, when the stock price exceeds the conversion price, bondholders have an incentive to delay conversion until forced redemption in order to benefit from further upside. Converting immediately would forfeit the remaining fixed-income component and the potential for additional gains.

3 Deep learning approximation in the backward recursion

In this section, we establish the theoretical foundation of the proposed deep learning-based numerical scheme for solving this path-dependent pricing problem. Starting from a discrete-time dynamic programming formulation, we use neural networks to approximate the conditional expectations involved in the backward recursion. We then analyze the convergence properties of the scheme, with particular focus on how discretization and approximation errors accumulate over time. We also provide detailed implementation, including the neural network approximation, training procedure, and overall numerical workflow, to ensure the method is reproducible.

4 Numerical Experiments

In this section, we conduct numerical experiments to evaluate the performance of the proposed deep learning-based pricing framework. We first compare pricing results generated under GBM, CEV, and Heston dynamics to assess the impact of model specification. We then analyze the impact of the downward reset and call provisions on bond values. Finally, we perform sensitivity analysis with respect to key contractual and market parameters.

To illustrate the practical relevance of our model, we consider a real-market convertible bond: the China CITIC Bank Convertible Bond (code: 113021), issued on March 4, 2019. It has a total issuance size of 40 billion yuan, with a face value of 100 yuan per unit, and was listed on the Shanghai Stock Exchange on March 19, 2019. The bond term spans from March 4, 2019 to March 3, 2025, and the conversion period runs from September 11, 2019 to March 3, 2025.

The contract incorporates two key path-dependent provisions. First, if the closing price of CITIC Bank’s A-shares is below 80% of the effective conversion price for at least 15 of any consecutive 30 trading days, the board of directors has the right to propose a downward revision of the conversion price, which is subject to shareholder approval and regulatory constraints. The revised conversion price shall not be lower than the average trading price of the A-shares on the 30 trading days, 20 trading days, and the most recent trading day, nor can it be lower than the most recently audited net asset value per share or the par value of the stock. Second, during the conversion period, if the closing price of CITIC Bank’s A-shares is not lower than the call barrier that is at least 130% of the then-effective conversion price for at least 15 out of 30 consecutive trading days, the issuer may redeem all or part of the unconverted convertible bonds at par value plus accrued interest for the period. 4.2 Effects of reset and call provisions

Table 3 quantifies the marginal effects of the call and reset provisions to the overall convertible bond value. Specifically, we compare three contract structures: a straight bond without embedded options, a bond with only a call provision, and a bond with both call and downward reset provisions. We address three questions: what is the value of a plain bond without any embedded provisions, how does the value change when a call provision is added, and how does it further change when a reset provision is also included?

Two robust and consistent patterns emerge across all model specifications. First, introducing the call provision reduces the bond value. Second, incorporating the reset provision on top of the call provision leads to a further decline in value. These relationships hold consistently under GBM, CEV, and Heston dynamics, suggesting that the observed effects are driven by the contractual structure rather than the choice of underlying dynamics.

Quantitatively, the call effect ranges from approximately −5.7-5.7 to −8.5-8.5, indicating a substantial impact. This is in line with standard intuition: the call provision effectively imposes an upper bound on the continuation value, allowing the issuer to redeem the bond when its value raises sufficiently, thereby truncating the investor’s upside potential. In essence, the embedded option component is capped. Across models, this effect is strongest under GBM and somewhat weaker under Heston, which reflect the richer distribution of price paths under stochastic volatility, where high-value states are more dispersed and the call constraint is, on average, less binding.

At first glance, the reset provision, by lowering the conversion price, would be expected to increase the bond value. However, the results show a negative reset effect, ranging from approximately −4.5-4.5 to −5.3-5.3 across all models. This is not a numerical artifact, but reflects a key institutional feature of convertible bond contracts common in the Chinese market. The call trigger is defined relative to the current conversion price, i.e., S≥b​HtS\geq bH_{t}, where HtH_{t} is updated after a reset. As a result, the reset provision generates two opposing effects. On the one hand, a lower HtH_{t} increases the conversion value and is beneficial to investors. On the other hand, it also lowers the effective call threshold b​HtbH_{t}, making early redemption more likely and limiting future upside. Under the parameter configuration of the CITIC Bank convertible bond, the latter effect dominates. 4.3 Path-dependent state structure and decision regions

To better understand how path-dependent features affect the optimal conversion decision, we examine the joint behaviour of the key state variables. Specifically, we consider the interaction between the call counter NcallN^{\mathrm{call}}, which captures short-term trigger conditions, and the cumulative number of reset events NresetN^{\mathrm{reset}}, which reflects longer-term path history. Since contractual features play a more important role in valuation than the specification of the underlying stock dynamics, we use the GBM model throughout Figure 3. This figure displays cross-sectional slices of simulated paths at several selected time points. Each point represents a state (Ncall,cumulative resets)(N^{\mathrm{call}},\text{cumulative resets}) together with the corresponding optimal decision. This representation makes it possible to track how the decision regions evolve over time and how they are shaped by the interaction between call and reset dynamics. We use a discrete-time approximation with 252 time steps per year. The continuation value functions are estimated using 12,00012{,}000 simulated training paths, while 4,0004{,}000 independent paths are used for evaluation. For the state-space visualizations, 5,0005{,}000 simulated paths are generated to construct cross-sectional slices at selected time points. All parameters are fixed at the baseline values reported in Table 2.

Figure 3 shows a clear negative relationship between the stock price StS_{t} and the cumulative number of reset events. Paths associated with persistently weak stock prices tend to accumulate more reset events over time. Although each reset lowers the conversion price HtH_{t} and therefore reduces the future reset threshold, sustained low stock-price dynamics may still generate repeated resets during the life of the bond. Moreover, repeated resets may indirectly increase the likelihood of satisfying call-related conversion conditions. Since a lower conversion price increases the conversion value ratio St/HtS_{t}/H_{t}, the convertible bond becomes more deeply in the money after multiple resets, making conversion and issuer call triggers more likely in later periods.

In a standard convertible bond without path-dependent features, the optimal conversion decision is mainly driven by the comparison between the stock price and the conversion price. Conversion typically occurs only when the stock price is sufficiently high relative to the conversion price, reflecting the intrinsic value of the embedded option. Moreover, due to the time value of the conversion option, even when the stock price exceeds the conversion price, immediate conversion is often suboptimal, and investors tend to delay exercise. By contrast, the presence of path-dependent contractual features changes this behaviour. In our framework, repeated reset events gradually reduce the effective conversion price, while the call provision introduces an additional constraint once the trigger condition is met. As a result, conversion may occur even when the contemporaneous stock price is not particularly high in absolute terms. This does not reflect a change in the intrinsic payoff structure, but rather the cumulative effect of past price movements and contractual triggers.

Overall, when both reset and call provisions are present, the optimal conversion policy can no longer be characterized by a simple threshold in the stock price. Instead, it depends on the joint configuration of multiple path-dependent state variables. This is also evident in the state-space visualization, where similar levels of the stock price can be associated with different optimal decisions depending on the accumulated reset history and the degree of proximity to the call trigger.

4.4 Sensitivity analysis

In this section, we examine the sensitivity of convertible bond prices to key model and contract parameters, including the initial underlying stock price, volatility and call threshold ratio. The analysis is performed under the GBM, CEV, and Heston model specifications to assess the robustness of patterns under different dynamics. We show that the impacts of key parameters are highly consistent across all three models, confirming that contractual structure and payoff design dominate the choice of diffusion process in determining convertible bond values.

Tables 4–6 report the price changes and relative precentages deviations under perturbations to each parameter while keeping others fixed at the baseline values.

Across all models, the initial underlying stock price S0S_{0} appears as the dominant driver of bond value. Under GBM and CEV, increasing the volatility σ\sigma from 0.15 to 0.40 raises the bond value by only about 2.86% and 2.04%, respectively, which is small compared to the effect of S0S_{0}. Among contractual terms, the reset parameter aa has a limited and generally non-monotonic impact. The overall sensitivity patterns are remarkably consistent across GBM, CEV and Heston models. The dominant role of S0S_{0}, the relatively modest influence of volatility σ\sigma, and the asymmetric effects of the call and reset threshold ratios aa and bb are all preserved under these three dynamics. This implies that contractual features and payoff structure play a more critical role than the specification of price dynamics in determining convertible bond values.

5 Conclusion

This paper develops a deep learning-based framework for pricing convertible bonds with downward reset and call provisions in a path-dependent setting. By formulating the valuation problem as a path-dependent PPDE, we explicitly capture the dependence of contractual triggers on the historical trajectory of the underlying stock price. The proposed numerical scheme combines a discrete-time dynamic programming representation with neural network approximation of conditional expectations, providing a flexible and scalable approach for high-dimensional problems.

Within this unified framework, we derive model-specific PPDE formulations under GBM, CEV, and Heston dynamics, and implement a backward learning algorithm to approximate the value functions. The numerical results demonstrate that the method produces stable and accurate pricing outcomes across all model specifications. In particular, the resulting pricing surfaces exhibit clear and economically intuitive structure, reflecting the hybrid nature of convertible bonds as a combination of debt and embedded equity options.

Our analysis highlights several robust and meaningful findings. First, the convertible bond value is primarily driven by the underlying stock price, exhibiting a strong nonlinear and convex response that aligns with the option-like payoff structure. Second, the impact of volatility is positive but relatively modest, as contractual features such as the bond floor and call provision attenuate the standard convexity effect. Third, the call provision has a significant negative effect on valuation by truncating upside potential. Finally, our most counter-intuitive finding is that the reset provision, despite improving conversion terms, actually further reduces value by lowering the effective call threshold and increasing the probability of early redemption. These effects are remarkably consistent across GBM, CEV, and Heston dynamics, suggesting that contractual design plays a more dominant role than the specific choice of diffusion process.

Taken together, these results indicate that path-dependent contractual features are central to understanding convertible bond valuation, especially in markets such as China where reset and call provisions are prevalent. This PPDE-based deep learning approach provides a natural and efficient framework to accommodate these complex features.

Several directions for future research remain open. First, extending the framework to incorporate credit risk would enable a more realistic treatment of default and recovery. Second, computational efficiency can be further enhanced by developing surrogate models that approximate full pricing functions across broad parameter ranges. Finally, integrating the proposed methodology with more advanced machine learning architectures may improve the ability to capture complex nonlinear interactions in high-dimensional pricing environments.
